Before any lens was chosen, the patient had to be heard.

Meet the Patient put three patients into two consult rooms on the Alcon stand. None of them gave away their life, their priorities or what they wanted from surgery unless the surgeon asked. Over three days surgeons sat down for 42 full consults, at a median of five and a half minutes each. This report covers how the patients were made, how the rooms ran and what the numbers show.

Act 1 The brief

The brief was patient first.

Get surgeons to take a history before they choose a lens, then hand them to the IOL Manager with something to talk about.

So the patients were written to hold back. Each one opens with a greeting and nothing more. Their daily life, what they want from surgery and what they would trade away only come out when the surgeon asks for them.

A surgeon who skips the history still has to make a recommendation, and the patient tells them, politely, when it does not fit the life they have just described.

Act 2 The patients

Three real people, cast for the part.

Peter, Margaret and Rami were cast against Alcon’s three patient profiles and filmed in a Melbourne studio in October. The face and voice of each patient come from the actor’s own recording, so the person on the screen looks and sounds like someone a surgeon would see in clinic.

Alcon owns the recordings of the three actors and holds the releases, written for indefinite reuse.

Clinical details and lens mapping sit with Alcon Medical Affairs and are not shown here. Each card shows the conversation design: who the patient is, and what the surgeon has to draw out.

Act 3 The rooms

Two private rooms, run from a phone.

Each room had a 55″ portrait screen with the patient at seated eye level, a headset with a boom mic and a chair. The IOL Manager chose the patient and started each consult from a phone, so nobody on the stand touched the equipment in the wall. Between consults the screen held a waiting-room loop.

Every patient was filmed on green and shown on Alcon blue, so the rooms carry the brand without a set build.

Both rooms were monitored for the three days, and adjustments during show hours were made remotely, without opening the stand.

Act 4 Nothing recorded

Nothing a surgeon said was recorded.

What the system keeps

The room, the patient chosen, the start time and the length of each consult. This report is built from that and nothing else.

What it never had

No transcript, no audio, no video and no names. Each conversation is deleted from the platform as soon as the consult ends.

That matters when a surgeon is speaking to a stranger at a trade stand. It also means everything qualitative in this report comes from the people who were in the room.

Act 5 The numbers

Every consult on the stand, end to end.

Each column is one consult. The dashed line is the time limit: seven minutes on day one, raised to ten for days two and three because consults kept running into it.

What the numbers show

42 consults ran a minute or longer, with a median of five minutes 38 seconds. 13 ran until the time limit ended them, the longest at ten minutes seven seconds on the Sunday.

What is counted

54 sessions were logged across the three days. The 12 under a minute were warm-ups and resets between surgeons. They are shown in outline and left out of the median. Day three was still running when this was generated.

Act 7 Next

What happens next.

RANZCO used the patients as a stand. They will hold the same consult with anyone who sits down, and Alcon owns the recordings they are made from.

We would like to sit down with Alcon and work through where they go next: the next congress, Alcon’s own team, or surgeons between meetings.
